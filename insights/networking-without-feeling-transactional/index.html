Networking Without Feeling Transactional

For many experienced professionals, the word networking creates an immediate sense of discomfort.

It can sound forced.

Self-promotional.

Transactional.

Like turning every conversation into an attempt to extract something from someone else.

That is not how I think useful professional relationships work.

Good networking is not about collecting contacts or asking people for favours.

It is about staying connected, being curious, exchanging useful information and building enough trust that conversations can lead somewhere over time.

Why networking often feels uncomfortable

The discomfort usually comes from the way networking is framed.

People are often told to:

  • reach out to as many people as possible

  • tell everyone they are looking for work

  • ask for introductions

  • request coffee meetings

  • “work the room”

  • follow up relentlessly.

For some people that approach may work.

For many experienced professionals, it feels unnatural.

And when it feels unnatural, the conversation often becomes awkward for both sides.

The problem is not relationships.

The problem is approaching relationships only when you need something.

Start with curiosity, not an ask

A better conversation often begins with genuine curiosity.

You may want to understand:

  • what is changing in a sector

  • how a particular organisation is thinking about an issue

  • where demand is increasing

  • what skills are becoming more important

  • how someone else is seeing the market

  • what challenges leaders are dealing with.

That creates a very different dynamic.

Instead of saying:

“Can you help me find a job?”

you are saying:

“I value your perspective and would like to understand what you are seeing.”

That is a more natural conversation.

It also tends to produce better information.

Relationships are not a short-term tactic

One of the biggest mistakes people make in career transition is treating networking as a project that starts when they lose a job and stops when they find one.

Professional relationships are more useful when they are part of normal working life.

That does not mean constant activity.

It simply means staying in touch with people you respect.

Checking in occasionally.

Sharing something useful.

Congratulating someone on a move.

Making an introduction when it helps.

Having a conversation because you are interested, not because you need an outcome.

Over time, that creates trust.

And trust is what makes relationships useful when important conversations arise.

You do not need a huge network

There is also a tendency to think that networking success depends on having thousands of connections.

It usually does not.

A smaller group of relevant, trusted relationships can be far more valuable than a large list of weak contacts.

Think about the people who:

  • understand your work

  • know your reputation

  • have seen you perform

  • operate in markets you care about

  • are connected to organisations or people relevant to your direction

  • are willing to have an honest conversation with you.

Those relationships often matter more than raw network size.

Be clear about what you are exploring

Networking becomes difficult when you cannot explain what you are interested in.

If your message is:

“I’m open to anything.”

people have very little to work with.

They cannot easily think of relevant organisations, people or opportunities.

You do not need to have every detail solved.

But you should be able to communicate enough direction for someone else to understand the kind of work, problems, sectors or environments you are exploring.

Clarity makes conversations easier.

It also makes introductions more useful.

Ask better questions

The quality of a networking conversation is often determined by the quality of the questions.

Instead of asking:

“Do you know of any jobs?”

you might ask:

  • What are you seeing in the market at the moment?

  • Where do you think organisations are struggling?

  • Which capabilities seem to be becoming more important?

  • Who else do you think would have an interesting perspective on this?

  • If you were in my position, what would you be paying attention to?

These questions invite insight rather than obligation.

And sometimes the most useful outcome of a conversation is not an opportunity.

It is a piece of information that changes your direction.

Give something back

Professional relationships work best when they are reciprocal.

That does not mean keeping score.

It means thinking about how you can also be useful.

You may have:

  • information

  • experience

  • introductions

  • perspective

  • encouragement

  • industry knowledge

  • a useful article or resource.

Often the best way to strengthen a relationship is simply to be someone worth staying connected to.

Do not force the next step

Not every conversation needs to lead somewhere immediately.

Some will.

Many will not.

That is normal.

The goal is not to turn every interaction into an opportunity.

It is to stay part of the professional conversation.

Over time, people remember people who are thoughtful, credible, useful and easy to engage with.

That matters because many opportunities begin with a simple thought:

“I know someone who might be worth speaking to.”

A more useful definition of networking

If the word networking still feels uncomfortable, redefine it.

Think of it as:

maintaining professional relationships and having useful conversations.

That is really what it is.

No performance.

No pretending.

No forced self-promotion.

Just staying connected to people, being interested in what is happening around you, and making it easier for useful conversations to occur.

That kind of networking does not have to feel transactional.

Done well, it feels like what strong professional relationships have always been.
