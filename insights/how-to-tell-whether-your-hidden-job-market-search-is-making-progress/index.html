How to Tell Whether Your Hidden Job Market Search Is Making Progress

The hidden job market can be frustrating because progress is often indirect.

There may be no application stage, no interview status, no recruiter update and no neat sequence of steps. You can have several useful conversations and still have nothing that looks like a job opportunity.

That can make it feel as if nothing is happening.

But progress often appears in the quality of the information, introductions and conversations you are having before it appears as an interview or an offer.

The challenge is knowing how to recognise it.

Look for changes in how people understand you

One useful sign is that people begin describing your value more accurately.

A former colleague who previously introduced you as “an experienced operations leader” now says you would be worth speaking to about integrating two businesses.

A recruiter who associated you with one sector starts considering you for a different brief because they understand how your experience applies.

Someone repeats your explanation of the problems you solve back to you, in language that makes sense to them.

These changes suggest your positioning is becoming clearer. People have more to work with when they think about where you might fit or who you should meet.

You have moved beyond being someone with a substantial career history. They can see a reason to involve you in a particular conversation.

Notice when conversations become more specific

Early conversations may be broad. You are exploring a sector, testing your thinking or finding out what is changing.

As you learn more, your questions should become more focused.

You begin to understand which organisations face problems you can help solve. You discover who influences decisions, what has already been tried and where there may be a need for different capability.

The questions coming back to you may also change.

“What are you looking for?” becomes “Have you dealt with this kind of situation before?”

That is a useful shift. The other person is beginning to connect your experience with something concrete.

It still may not lead to a role. But it gives you a better basis for deciding what to explore next.

Better access can be progress

Consider a hypothetical example.

An operations executive is exploring opportunities in businesses that have grown through acquisition. Their first conversations are with former colleagues who know the sector.

One conversation reveals that integrating systems and teams is becoming a recurring problem. Another identifies an organisation struggling with exactly that issue.

A contact then introduces the executive to someone involved in the integration work.

There is still no vacancy. But the executive now understands a relevant business problem and has access to someone closer to it.

That is more useful than another general conversation about being available for work.

Learning where to stop is useful too

Progress does not always mean adding another organisation to your list.

You may discover that a business has little appetite for change, that the likely role would not suit you, or that your experience is less relevant there than you first thought.

That information helps you make better choices about where to spend your time.

A search can become more focused because you have ruled things out.

Activity alone is not enough

None of this means treating every coffee, connection or friendly response as evidence that an opportunity is approaching.

People can be encouraging without understanding your value. You can have a busy diary while repeatedly covering the same ground.

If conversations keep ending with “Send me your CV and I’ll keep an eye out”, look at what needs to change.

Are you being specific enough about your direction? Are you speaking with people who understand your target market? Are you using what you learn to improve the next conversation?

The aim is to see whether your activity is producing better information, clearer positioning or more relevant access.

Review what has changed

Every fortnight, ask yourself:

  • Can I explain my direction more clearly than I could two weeks ago?

  • Are people understanding and describing my value more accurately?

  • Do I know more about the problems my target organisations face?

  • Are introductions bringing me closer to people who understand or influence those problems?

  • Have I identified a useful next step—or a route I should stop pursuing?

Write down the changes and decide what to do with them.

A sharper introduction may suggest your positioning is working. Better information may justify pursuing one organisation more closely. Repeatedly vague conversations may mean you need to revisit your direction or who you are speaking with.

An offer remains the outcome you want. These earlier signals help you judge whether your approach is taking you closer to it.
